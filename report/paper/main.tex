\begin{abstract}
Vietnam's stock market is among the most retail-driven in the region, yet
ordinary investors must reconcile technical, fundamental and news signals across
fragmented, largely English-centric tools. We present \emph{Stockrium}, an
agentic decision-support system for Vietnamese equities that unifies these signals
with recommendation generation. Its core is a multi-agent LangGraph pipeline: an
orchestrator dispatches, in parallel, technical, fundamental and news-sentiment
agents whose outputs an aggregator fuses into a buy/wait recommendation with
horizon-dependent risk targets. The language model interprets the evidence and
proposes an action, while deterministic Python calculates confidence and may
downgrade a candidate buy when measurable safeguards fail, making the decision
path inspectable and conservatively constrained. The pipeline is
configurable: users enable or disable each agent and choose the indicators and
fundamental metric groups that drive it, making it a flexible testbed for their
own strategies rather than a rigid recommender. Backtesting on VN30 \footnote{VN30: the index of the $30$
largest and most-liquid stocks on the Ho Chi Minh Stock Exchange (HOSE).} over a
three-year window shows the system behaves as designed: far more selective than
the unfiltered technical engine, reducing completed trades by $64.0\%$ and mean
maximum drawdown by $9.93$ percentage points, but also producing lower median
return and win rate. Its median trade-level Sharpe statistic is modestly higher,
and it almost never trades into downtrends. As the window is predominantly bullish
and lightly traded, we characterize the workflow as a conservative exposure filter
rather than a statistically established return edge. The
system is decision-support only and confined to Vietnamese equities.

\keywords{Agentic AI \and Large language models \and Multi-agent systems \and
Investment decision support \and Vietnamese
stock market}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Vietnam's stock market has a large retail base: by the end of 2025 domestic
investors held about $11.9$ million securities accounts (some $11.6\%$ of the
population) and around $90\%$ of daily trading
value~\cite{vsd2025,vinacapital2024}, ahead of FTSE Russell
's coming upgrade to
Secondary Emerging~\cite{ftse2025}. Yet the market is led by experienced foreign
and proprietary desks toward whom retail investors herd~\cite{vnherd2022}; the
non-professionals forming most of the volume lack experience, are prone to
losses~\cite{barber2013}, and follow KOLs\footnote{Key Opinion Leaders} in online groups~\cite{vnfomo} with no
way to backtest a strategy of their own.

Existing tools do little to help: consumer apps such as SSI give generic,
black-box tips, professional platforms such as TradingView cover only technical
analysis, and across all of them the technical, fundamental and news lenses stay
fragmented in largely English-centric tools. What is needed is a tool that
reconciles these sources in Vietnamese and returns a clear, explainable
recommendation a user can trust, adapt and test. A naive LLM cannot fill the
gap---it hallucinates and is hard to reproduce or explain when real money is at
stake---so a hybrid design is needed: the model interprets evidence and produces
a structured candidate recommendation, while explicit, auditable rules calculate
indicators and confidence and enforce measurable, safety-sensitive decision
constraints.

We present \emph{Stockrium}, a multi-agent LangGraph pipeline for Vietnamese
equities: an orchestrator dispatches, in parallel, a technical, a fundamental and
a news-sentiment agent whose outputs an aggregator fuses into a buy/wait
recommendation with risk-managed targets, under one principle: \emph{the large
language model analyzes the evidence and proposes a candidate action, while
deterministic rules validate it and keep the final result within safe risk bounds}.
It is configurable rather than rigid: the
user toggles agents and picks the indicators and fundamental groups that feed the
analysis, serving individual investors as a unified recommender across the three
lenses. A separate internal web interface gives authorized evaluators a
chart-based testbed for controlled analysis and historical backtesting.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item A multi-agent LangGraph framework unifying technical, fundamental and news
analysis with recommendation for Vietnamese equities, with three analysis agents
running in parallel over a shared state and an aggregator producing the final
buy/wait decision.
\item A fusion-and-decision mechanism: confidence renormalized over active sources,
plus a hybrid LLM/rule decision with horizon-dependent risk bounds and conservative
technical, fundamental, news and confidence vetoes, keeping verdicts inspectable.
\item A configurable design letting users toggle agents and select indicators and
fundamental metric groups, a flexible testbed for their own strategies.
\item A purpose-built Vietnamese backtesting tool that overlays trades on the price
chart and evaluates strategies with significance testing, extensible to more
advanced AI algorithms.
\item A curated prototype dataset for the Vietnamese market covering all three
lenses---price and volume history, company fundamentals, and news with sentiment
labels---as the basis for a future versioned release with stronger point-in-time
controls and documentation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.}
The system is \emph{decision-support} only: it produces analysis and
recommendations but does \emph{not} execute orders, connect to a brokerage, or
manage portfolios, and its output is not financial advice. Its coverage is
confined to Vietnamese equities, from which all data, tickers and indices are
drawn.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Stockrium builds on four strands of prior work.

\emph{Multi-agent LLM systems for trading.} The closest is
TradingAgents~\cite{tradingagents}, which casts a trading firm as a society of LLM
agents---analysts, bull/bear researchers, a trader and a risk team---that
communicate through structured outputs and natural-language debate, reporting gains
on US equities over single-agent and rule baselines. Its role specialization and
structured communication inform our design, but it runs on US-centric data and lets
the agents debate their way to the decision, which is costly and sensitive to
prompt and sampling variation. FinMem~\cite{finmem} instead gives a single LLM
trader a layered memory; like TradingAgents, it keeps the decision inside the
model. Stockrium adapts the multi-agent idea to the Vietnamese market but places
deterministic scoring and validation around the LLM's candidate recommendation and
adds per-agent and per-indicator configuration.

\emph{Finance-specific LLMs.} BloombergGPT~\cite{bloomberggpt} and the open-source
FinGPT~\cite{fingpt} improve financial NLP such as sentiment classification and
summarization, but they are analytical \emph{assistants}: they neither fuse
multiple lenses nor produce risk-managed, backtested recommendations, and none
targets Vietnamese equities. We therefore use a strong general model for the
language tasks and build the decision logic around it.

\emph{Financial news sentiment analysis.} FinBERT and its
successors~\cite{finbert2023} and FinBERT-LSTM models~\cite{finbertlstm2022} turn
financial text into a sentiment signal that aids price prediction, but they are
opaque one-step predictors, not an explainable recommendation that reconciles
sentiment with technical and fundamental evidence---the role of our news agent.

\emph{The Vietnamese market.} PhoBERT-based classifiers label stock-article
sentiment well~\cite{phobertstock}, and studies report significant changes in return
variance around negative news but no statistically significant change in mean
returns~\cite{vnnews2023}; behavioural work documents herding and FOMO among newer
investors~\cite{vnherd2022,vnfomo}; for price forecasting, LSTM, BiLSTM and
Transformer models and conventional machine learning have been applied to the
VN-Index~\cite{vndl2025,vnlstm2021,vnml2024}. This work is largely single-source
and predictive, rarely LLM-based or risk-calibrated, and offers no interactive tool
for fusing signals and backtesting.

To our knowledge no prior system simultaneously (i)~unifies technical, fundamental
and news analysis with recommendation for the Vietnamese market, (ii)~uses a
hybrid LLM/rule design that stays explainable and inspectable, (iii)~enforces
horizon-dependent risk constraints, and (iv)~provides a configurable, chart-based
backtesting testbed. This paper addresses that gap.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
Stockrium follows a client-server architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). On the
client side, a mobile application and a web application serve complementary roles.
The mobile application focuses on the core, everyday functions: displaying market
information and delivering per-stock analysis and recommendations to the user. The
web application focuses on backtesting and on analysis across one or several
tickers, letting authorized evaluators configure an analysis (choosing which agents,
technical indicators and fundamental metric groups to use), request recommendations,
and inspect backtests with the simulated trades drawn on the price chart. Both clients
share the same server boundary, but use different presentation technologies: the
mobile application is built with React Native and Expo, whereas the internal web
interface uses HTML, CSS and JavaScript. On the server side, a core service,
implemented with FastAPI in Python,
exposes the application's main functions through an API and coordinates three
components: a database, a managed PostgreSQL instance hosted on Supabase, that
stores the market, fundamental and news data; the multi-agent layer
(Section~\ref{sec:framework}), built with LangGraph, that performs the analysis;
and a connection to an external LLM provider used by the agents. Keeping a thin
client separate from a server that owns the data, the agents and the model access
places the heavy analysis and the model credentials on the server, while the
clients stay lightweight and serve distinct, complementary roles.

\section{The Multi-Agent Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

Given a ticker and risk profile, a five-node LangGraph \texttt{StateGraph}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}) fuses technical, fundamental and news evidence: an
orchestrator plans; three specialists run in parallel; and an aggregator synthesizes
their evidence. Within aggregation, the LLM produces a structured candidate
recommendation, while explicit rules compute indicator scores, confidence and risk
bounds and validate or downgrade the candidate before the final result. Through
\texttt{data\_selection}, users enable sources and choose technical indicators and
fundamental groups, making the graph a testbed for configurable strategies.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig1_framework}
\caption{The multi-agent analysis pipeline}
\label{fig:framework}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Orchestrator Agent}
\label{sec:orchestrator}

The orchestrator translates a free-form request and the investor's risk profile
into a machine-readable \emph{plan} for the downstream agents, using structured
decoding (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} at temperature $0$) so the plan is always a valid
Pydantic object. The risk profile's \texttt{period}
(\texttt{short\_term}/\texttt{mid\_term}/\texttt{long\_term}, defaulting to
\texttt{mid\_term}) determines a planning window of approximately $60$ days for
\texttt{short\_term}, $180$--$365$ days for \texttt{mid\_term}, and at least $730$
days for \texttt{long\_term}. After the model returns the dates, Python overwrites
the technical interval deterministically: daily candles for short-term, weekly
candles for medium-term and monthly candles for long-term analysis. The database
stores minute-level candles alongside daily ones and aggregates daily records for
weekly and monthly requests. Automatic mode applies the default selection and
horizon weights; manual mode retains the user's source switches and validated
weights while using the same planning graph.

\subsection{Technical Analysis Agent}
\label{sec:technical}

The technical agent computes in NumPy/Pandas RSI ($14$), SMA ($20/50$), Bollinger
Bands ($20$, $2\sigma$), MACD ($12/26/9$) and KDJ ($9/3/3$), covering momentum,
trend, volatility/mean reversion and stochastic oscillation~\cite{murphy1999}.
Rules in Table~\ref{tab:scoring} convert each enabled indicator $i$ into a bullish
score $s_i\in\{0,1\}$, favoring fresh upward momentum over extended readings. Raw
values and a justification accompany every score. The agent reports
$S_{\mathrm{tech}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}s_i$ and
$S_{\max}=|\mathcal{E}|$ for the user-enabled set $\mathcal{E}$ (all five by
default), allowing normalization regardless of how many indicators are active.

\subsection{Fundamental Analysis Agent}
\label{sec:fundamental}

The fundamental agent loads indicators, financial statements, stored summaries,
company profiles and ICB classifications, retaining up to five annual records in
chronological order. It computes CAGR where valid and, for non-financial firms,
organizes evidence into liquidity, leverage, efficiency, profitability and
valuation; DuPont components and annual ICB-industry medians provide trend and peer
context. For ICB 8xxx financial firms it instead presents available CAMELS-related
measures, uses P/B rather than relying on P/E or EV/EBITDA, and explicitly marks
unavailable specialist fields. Missing ticker data yields a ``no data'' result
rather than aborting a batch. The aggregator interprets this evidence as
\textsf{fundamental\_health} (strong/neutral/weak/N/A).

% ============================================================
\subsection{Article Analysis Agent}
\label{sec:news}

The news pipeline first stores source articles and enriches them offline with a
typed short Vietnamese summary and positive/neutral/negative sentiment, retaining
the original evidence and stock links. Online, the agent retrieves linked articles
within the planned interval and gives \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} their date, title,
summary and stored sentiment. At temperature $0.2$, it returns the main information,
positive points and risks, or ``no useful news.'' Disabled and empty sources are
handled gracefully; the aggregator derives \textsf{article\_sentiment}
(positive/neutral/negative/N/A).

\subsection{Aggregator Agent}
\label{sec:aggregator}
The aggregator fuses the three streams into one recommendation.
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} reads the agent outputs and emits a structured
\texttt{InvestmentRecommendation} with a summarization and a proposed verdict,
entry, take-profit, stop-loss and holding period, plus a normalized technical score
and qualitative fundamental and news labels. Only clearly adverse labels
(\textsf{weak} or \textsf{negative}) act as qualitative vetoes.

Confidence is the weighted mean of the three signals normalized to $[0,1]$.
Default technical/fundamental/news weights are $0.60/0.10/0.30$ (short term),
$0.40/0.40/0.20$ (medium) and $0.15/0.70/0.15$ (long); validated manual weights may
replace them. Python detects absent data and renormalizes weights over active
sources instead of treating missing evidence as neutral.

Python never promotes the model's candidate \textsf{Wait}. It retains a candidate
\textsf{Buy} only when the technical score reaches $60\%$ of its attainable maximum
(rounded up: $3$ of $5$ by default), neither qualitative veto fires, and confidence
exceeds $0.55$; otherwise it returns \textsf{Wait}. Surviving entry, take-profit,
stop-loss and holding values are clamped to horizon-dependent bounds. The prompt
targets reward-to-risk of at least $1.5$, but post-processing does not recheck it
after clamping, so this is not a final guarantee. Unrecovered failures safely yield
a \textsf{Wait}-shaped result.

\subsection{Agent Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

Having described what each agent computes, we now describe how they coordinate:
the shared communication protocol, the types of interaction between agents, and
the backbone LLMs. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} traces one analysis request through
the pipeline.

\subsubsection{Shared state and communication protocol.}
The agents do not call one another directly; they communicate through a single
typed state object, \texttt{AgentState} (a \texttt{TypedDict}), that flows through
the graph. It carries the execution mode, the user input, the risk profile, the
target symbol, a \texttt{data\_selection} object (per-source and per-indicator
toggles), the orchestrator's plan, the collected \texttt{agent\_results}, and the
final output. Every agent that consults the LLM does so through \emph{structured
decoding}: the model must return a valid instance of a Pydantic schema (the plan,
the extracted signals, the recommendation) rather than free text, so each message
exchanged in the graph is well-typed and machine-consumable rather than parsed
heuristically. The \texttt{agent\_results} field is annotated with an
\texttt{operator.or\_} reducer, letting the parallel analysis agents each write
their partial result into the shared dictionary without overwriting one another.

\subsubsection{Types of agent interactions.}
Three interaction patterns compose the pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}). First,
\emph{planning}: the orchestrator reads the request and risk profile and emits
parameters for every downstream agent, so the analysis agents receive a consistent
time window and interval. Second, \emph{running the analysis agents in parallel}: the
technical, fundamental and article agents run at the same time and independently,
and their results are combined once all of them finish, so no agent waits on
another and a missing source does not stall the rest. Third,
\emph{veto-based aggregation}: the aggregator consumes the merged results and,
rather than averaging opaque scores, applies the deterministic fusion and decision
rules of Section~\ref{sec:aggregator}, in which the technical score acts as the
trigger while the fundamental and news signals act as vetoes. A source that is
disabled or carries no data is detected by Python flags and dropped from the
computation, so the interaction degrades gracefully as sources come and go.

\subsubsection{Backbone LLMs.}
LLM-backed steps use structured output from OpenAI's \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}. The
orchestrator runs at temperature $0$, while article synthesis and aggregation run
at $0.2$. The model handles planning, semantic interpretation, evidence synthesis
and generation of a candidate recommendation. Python retains control over interval
selection, indicator calculation and scoring, confidence, veto conditions and
risk-bound enforcement. The aggregator caps its output tokens and retries once on
truncation; any unhandled error resolves to a safe \textsf{Wait}. This division
preserves the hybrid design: the LLM contributes financial interpretation and a
structured proposal, while deterministic code makes the numerical controls and
final safeguards auditable.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Backtesting is a central capability of Stockrium, serving its guiding purpose:
letting users experiment with and verify a strategy on historical data before
risking capital. We describe how the tool collects data, configures a strategy,
runs and scores a backtest, and visualizes and compares results.

\subsection{Data collection}
Experiments require historical data, which the application collects and maintains
itself rather than fetching live on demand. Daily and Intraday OHLCV\footnote{Open, High, Low, Close, Volume } data for the tracked
universe and the VN-Index (the benchmark) come from the SSI Fast Connect API \footnote{Source: https://guide.ssi.com.vn/ssi-products/tieng-viet/fastconnect-data} and are
stored as a rolling multi-year history; company fundamentals and Vietnamese
financial news are collected into the same store, the latter enriched offline into
per-article sentiment (Section~\ref{sec:news}). The saved full-pipeline aggregate
was accumulated one symbol at a time rather than produced as one atomic batch, and
it does not retain one immutable manifest containing the model, prompt, random seed
and data-snapshot versions. The experiment should therefore be treated as a saved
prototype artifact rather than a fully reproducible snapshot. It uses the VN30
constituents available when the experiment was executed ($30$ symbols) over a
three-year window, January 2023 to December 2025.

\subsection{Strategy configuration and comparison}
A \emph{strategy} in Stockrium is a configuration of the analysis: which agents
are enabled, which technical indicators and fundamental metric groups feed them,
and the risk horizon. An authorized evaluator sets this configuration, runs a backtest, and the
tool records the run together with its metrics; multiple runs can be stored and
compared side by side, so a user can see how, say, enabling the news agent, adding
an indicator, or changing the horizon affects performance. Two baselines are
available for reference: the unfiltered technical engine that drives the pipeline
(which trades on every technical signal, without the LLM fusion and vetoes) and a
passive buy-and-hold strategy.

\subsection{Backtesting procedure}
The backtest replays records in chronological order. Technical indicators at bar
$i$ use only data up to $i$, article evidence is bounded by the candidate date, and
a simulated entry fills at the open of bar $i{+}1$. The current fundamental branch,
however, loads the latest stored annual records without filtering them by filing
availability at the candidate date; full-pipeline results can therefore contain
fundamental look-ahead. To bound LLM cost, the pipeline is invoked only when the
vectorized historical engine's combined score crosses the threshold. It reuses the
production specialist nodes and structured aggregator, but supplies the technical
node with the historical indicator source. Some numerical conventions differ from
the live indicator service; the parity gate verifies scoring agreement when both
paths consume the same historical source, not numerical parity with live indicators.
A \textsf{Buy} opens a position that is simulated forward candle by candle until
take-profit, stop-loss or the holding limit is reached, and the realized return is
recorded.

\subsection{Performance metrics}
Each trade yields a net return $r=(p_{\mathrm{exit}}-p_{\mathrm{entry}})/
p_{\mathrm{entry}}-2c$, where $c=0.15\%$ is the per-side transaction cost. Over the
$n$ trades, the framework reports trade count, win rate, average and median trade
return, compounded and annualized return, maximum drawdown, trade-level Sharpe and
Calmar statistics, profit factor and exit counts. Evaluation also uses a one-sample
$t$-test against zero, a sign-permutation test, and the information coefficient
between confidence and forward return. Metrics are computed identically for both
baselines and pooled by market regime and confidence tier in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Trade visualization}
For inspection, the tool overlays every simulated trade on the price chart,
marking each entry and exit, so a user can see precisely where a strategy would
have bought and sold and judge its behaviour visually before committing real
capital (Fig.~\ref{fig:backtest}).

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We report results for the three-year evaluation window of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. The saved full-pipeline comparison and the
single-indicator ablations use different cross-sectional summaries; the caption of
Table~\ref{tab:indicators} states the aggregation used for each row group.

\subsection{Aggregate performance}
Table~\ref{tab:indicators} reports the three-year run for the full pipeline, the
unfiltered technical engine (all five indicators), each single indicator run in
isolation, and passive buy-and-hold. The pipeline's most consistent effect is
\emph{selectivity}: requiring a clear technical trigger and vetoing on weak
fundamentals or negative news, it takes far fewer positions than the engine
($272$ vs.\ $756$ trades, a $64.0\%$ reduction) and improves mean maximum
drawdown from $-27.04\%$ to $-17.11\%$. This conservatism comes with lower mean and
median return and lower win rate: median per-symbol total return falls from
$21.33\%$ to $12.31\%$, and mean win rate from $51.74\%$ to $45.42\%$. The
pipeline's median trade-level Sharpe statistic is modestly higher ($0.746$ vs.\
$0.637$), although sparse per-symbol trades make this comparison unstable.

The per-indicator rows also show that no single indicator dominates every
criterion. Bollinger Bands produce the highest mean per-symbol return ($47.11\%$),
while KDJ has the highest mean Sharpe statistic ($0.722$) and win rate ($52.11\%$).
Moving-average conditions create the fewest trades and the smallest mean drawdown
($-16.98\%$), but also the lowest mean return ($20.62\%$). This dispersion supports
the configurable design without establishing that equal binary weighting is
optimal.

\subsection{Benchmark comparison and regimes}
Against the unfiltered engine the pipeline reduced drawdown substantially while
improving total return on $14$ of $30$ symbols; across the universe, however, its
mean and median returns are lower. Against buy-and-hold it is less favourable, with
only $6$ of $30$ symbols beating it (median buy-and-hold $78.61\%$), as expected for a
selective, partially invested, long-only strategy over a bullish $2023$--$2025$
recovery, whose value lies instead in sitting out pullbacks (hence the smaller
drawdown); the median random-entry percentile is $71.45\%$, without a
multiple-comparison-adjusted universe-level test. By regime, it opened almost no
positions in downtrends (one of $272$ trades), concentrating on sideways ($232$
trades, $47.41\%$ win, $2.75\%$ mean return) and uptrending ($39$, $30.77\%$,
$1.21\%$) markets, a direct consequence of the
buy/wait logic.

\subsection{Calibration and statistical significance}
The three-year run lets us probe the confidence score and significance.
Medium-confidence trades outperform high-confidence trades in both pooled win rate
($48.65\%$ vs.\ $44.26\%$) and mean net return ($4.29\%$ vs.\ $2.21\%$), although
the medium group contains only $37$ observations compared with $235$ high-confidence
trades. There are no low-tier trades by design because low confidence maps to
\textsf{Wait}. Across symbols, the mean fraction of profitable walk-forward windows
is $14.44\%$ and the median is $14.29\%$. On significance, with an average of about
$9$ trades per symbol, a one-sample $t$-test against zero and a sign-permutation
test each reach $p<0.05$ for only $2$ of $30$ symbols. The mean score--return
information coefficient is $0.011$ and the median is $-0.034$, both indicating at
best a weak and statistically unreliable monotonic relationship. We
thus read the results as evidence the pipeline behaves as designed (selective,
drawdown-aware, downtrend-avoiding) rather than as a statistically established
return edge; because the window is predominantly bullish with few in-position
declines, establishing such an edge would need a larger universe and windows
spanning full bear markets.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The results are best read not as beating the market but as evidence the system
meets its goals of explainability and risk control: more selective than the
unfiltered engine, it reduces mean drawdown and modestly improves the median
trade-level Sharpe statistic, but produces lower mean and median return and win
rate. The evidence therefore supports interpreting it as a conservative exposure
filter rather than a broadly superior return strategy. These results are also an
early trial of a backtesting tool we built
ourselves rather than a definitive verdict, since any result depends on the user's
own choices of indicators and models; what we mainly show is the ability to run
backtests flexibly so a strategy can be judged in quantitative rather than purely
qualitative terms.

Several caveats apply: the window is predominantly bullish with few trades per
symbol, so significance tests are underpowered; the VN30 universe uses current
membership (survivorship bias); the fundamental branch can load records unavailable
at a historical candidate date; the aggregate lacks a common immutable run
manifest; the long-only strategy structurally lags buy-and-hold in rallies; returns
include transaction costs but not slippage or portfolio-level allocation; and the
pipeline depends on a proprietary LLM whose narratives may still err. The system
is decision-support only, not financial advice, and confined to Vietnamese
equities, though its auditable decision layer lets a user or regulator inspect any
recommendation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented an agentic decision-support system for Vietnamese equities that
unifies technical, fundamental and news-sentiment analysis with recommendation
generation. Its core is a multi-agent LangGraph pipeline (an orchestrator, three
parallel analysis agents and an aggregator) in which the language model interprets
evidence and proposes a recommendation, while deterministic code calculates
confidence and may reject a candidate buy that violates measurable controls. This
yields structured, inspectable buy/wait recommendations with active-source
renormalization and horizon-dependent risk targets. Demo videos of the mobile and web applications are
available online.\footnote{\url{https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1FN3j_Un-9gzbIFytVNeGLWqDyC-ZJYn8}}

Backtesting on VN30 over a three-year window shows the system behaves as designed:
markedly more selective than the unfiltered engine, reducing completed trades from
$756$ to $272$ and mean maximum drawdown from $-27.04\%$ to $-17.11\%$, while
almost never trading into downtrends. It also has lower return and win rate, despite
a modestly higher median trade-level Sharpe statistic. As the window is
predominantly bullish with few trades and point-in-time fundamental leakage, we do
not claim a statistically established return edge; the defensible interpretation
is a conservative, transparent exposure filter. Within its scope
(analysis only, no execution, Vietnamese equities alone), it serves individual
investors inspecting stock evidence and authorized evaluators testing the supported
pipeline configurations. Future work
targets longer, multi-regime evaluation over broader universes with point-in-time
membership, portfolio-level backtesting with slippage and exposure-adjusted
metrics, and better confidence calibration.
