\section{Artificial Intelligence Foundations}
\label{sec:artificial-intelligence-foundations}

Stockrium combines large language models, agentic AI orchestration, deterministic decision rules, and historical evaluation. This section introduces the artificial-intelligence and evaluation concepts required to understand the analytical workflow; their implementation is presented in Chapter 4.

\subsection{Large Language Models}

Large language models (LLMs) are neural models trained on extensive text collections to understand and generate natural language. Most modern LLMs are based on the Transformer architecture, which uses attention to represent relationships among words in a context \cite{vaswani2017attention}. Their instruction-following capability allows one model to summarize articles, interpret financial observations, classify content, and generate explanations without training a separate model for every task \cite{brown2020gpt3}. Nevertheless, LLMs can produce unsupported or inconsistent statements, and a valid response structure does not guarantee factual or numerical correctness. In Stockrium, LLMs are consequently used mainly for interpretation and explanation, while exact calculations and selected validation rules remain in conventional code.

\subsection{AI Agents, Agentic AI, and Coordination Workflows}

An artificial-intelligence (AI) agent combines a model with a defined role, available information, state, and actions such as calling an application programming interface (API) or returning a structured result. The Reasoning and Acting (ReAct) pattern illustrates how model reasoning can be interleaved with external actions and their observations \cite{yao2022react}. Agentic AI extends this idea into a goal-directed workflow in which model-guided planning, tool or data access, shared state, and deterministic controls cooperate to complete a bounded task. In Stockrium, one orchestrated analysis graph directs specialized technical, fundamental, and news nodes and exposes their intermediate evidence for later synthesis. This separation improves modularity but can also increase latency and propagate errors, making clear responsibilities and compatible output formats necessary.

Several common patterns organize agentic applications. A \textit{specialized node} owns one analytical domain; an \textit{orchestrator--worker} structure assigns work to the appropriate nodes; \textit{fan-out/fan-in} executes independent branches in parallel before joining them; and an \textit{aggregator} reconciles their outputs. Shared state provides a common data contract, while conditional routing selects the next node from the current state. Stockrium combines these patterns within one explicit and bounded workflow rather than relying on unrestricted component-to-component conversation.

An agentic AI workflow represents processing as a graph of nodes and transitions. Nodes may retrieve data, perform calculations, invoke an LLM, or update shared state, while edges define sequential, conditional, or parallel execution. LangGraph provides these graph and state abstractions together with checkpointing for persistent conversations \cite{langgraph2026graphapi}. In Stockrium, one graph coordinates the stock-analysis components and another routes chatbot requests. This structure makes the allowed execution paths visible and allows deterministic functions and language-model nodes to operate within the same workflow.

\subsection{Hybrid LLM--Rule Decision Support}
\label{subsec:hybrid-llm-rule-decision-support}

The term \textit{hybrid LLM--rule-based approach} describes an architecture that combines the complementary strengths of a large language model and an explicit rule-based component. The LLM processes information that requires language understanding, contextual interpretation, and natural-language explanation. The rule-based component performs calculations and applies conditions whose behavior must be deterministic, reproducible, and inspectable. In this thesis, the term is descriptive rather than the name of one specific published algorithm.

The approach is informed by several related research directions. Neuro-symbolic AI studies the integration of neural models with explicit knowledge and reasoning \cite{garcez2023neurosymbolic}. MRKL systems apply this modular principle to language models by connecting them with external knowledge and discrete reasoning modules \cite{karpas2022mrkl}. NeMo Guardrails demonstrates that interpretable, user-defined rules can be applied at runtime independently of the underlying LLM \cite{rebedea2023nemo}. DecisionFlow additionally reports that representing actions and constraints explicitly can improve decision accuracy and alignment on high-stakes benchmarks \cite{chen2025decisionflow}. Stockrium adopts the general separation-of-responsibilities principle from this literature; it does not reproduce any of these frameworks or claim that they directly define its financial rules.

In Stockrium, specialist components first prepare technical, fundamental, and financial-news evidence. The LLM-based aggregator interprets these inputs, resolves their qualitative implications, produces a candidate \textit{Buy} or \textit{Wait} assessment, and explains the result. Deterministic Python code then calculates the technical score and confidence, excludes unavailable evidence, and checks the candidate against explicit conditions. A candidate Buy is downgraded to Wait if the enabled technical indicators do not reach the required score, available fundamentals are classified as weak, available news is negative, or confidence is below the configured threshold. For an accepted Buy, code also constrains take-profit, stop-loss, and holding values according to the selected investment horizon. The complete formulas, thresholds, and boundaries are presented in Chapter~4.

This hybrid design is used because neither component is sufficient by itself. A purely rule-based system would be difficult to maintain for diverse news events, company circumstances, and conflicting qualitative evidence. A purely LLM-based system would be flexible but could produce unsupported statements, inconsistent decisions, or numerical errors \cite{ji2023hallucination}. Combining them allows the LLM to provide contextual interpretation while deterministic rules provide repeatability and a conservative verification layer. The particular Stockrium thresholds and weights are project-defined engineering choices rather than values derived directly from the cited papers, and the resulting confidence score should not be interpreted as a calibrated probability of profit.

\subsection{Backtesting and Evaluation Foundations}

Backtesting is the process of applying a defined decision rule or analytical pipeline to historical data and simulating the decisions that it would have produced at each point in time. It is needed because evaluating a financial-analysis workflow only through live use would be slow, costly, and difficult to reproduce. Historical simulation provides a controlled environment for examining whether the generated signals lead to acceptable outcomes, comparing configurations and baselines, and identifying weaknesses under different market conditions. In Stockrium, backtesting therefore acts as a feedback mechanism: its results help developers assess and refine the automated analysis flow that serves most users. These results describe how the flow behaved on past data under stated assumptions; they do not predict future performance or guarantee profitability.

A meaningful backtest must specify its signal, entry, exit, holding-period, and transaction-cost assumptions while preventing the use of information that was unavailable at the simulated decision time. Its outcome is then summarized using evaluation measures. In this thesis, total return compounds successive closed-trade returns as $\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1+r_i)-1$ for each security; win rate is the proportion of the $N$ closed trades with a positive net return; and maximum drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough decline in the simulated equity curve. Trade count is also reported because percentages derived from very few observations are less reliable.

The Sharpe ratio generally measures excess return per unit of return variability \cite{sharpe1994ratio}. Stockrium's implementation uses closed-trade returns, a zero risk-free benchmark, and computes $\sqrt{N}\,\bar{r}/s_r$, where $\bar{r}$ and $s_r$ are the sample mean and sample standard deviation of trade returns. It returns zero when fewer than two trades are available or when their returns have no variation. The result is therefore a trade-level comparative statistic rather than a conventional annualized daily-return Sharpe ratio. Simple baselines provide reference behavior, market-regime analysis separates results under different broad market trends, and walk-forward evaluation repeats testing over successive time windows instead of relying on one fixed split. Nevertheless, historical results can be distorted by overfitting, survivorship bias, look-ahead bias, incomplete data, and simplified execution assumptions \cite{bailey2017pbo}; they describe past behavior and do not prove future profitability. Stockrium's backtesting facility is consequently an internal evaluation tool for its pipeline and supported configurations, not a general-purpose strategy-construction platform.

Together with the financial-analysis foundations in Section~\ref{sec:financial-analysis-foundations}, these concepts explain the principal division of responsibilities in Stockrium: financial methods define the evidence, LLMs interpret it, the agentic AI workflow coordinates specialized work, deterministic rules constrain the result, and backtesting evaluates historical behavior.
